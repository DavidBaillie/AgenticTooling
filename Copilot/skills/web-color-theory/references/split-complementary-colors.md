# Split-Complementary Colors

## Definition

A split-complementary palette uses one base hue and the two hues adjacent to its complement. It provides strong contrast with less direct tension than a simple complementary palette.

## Formula

For a base hue `H`, the complement is approximately `H + 180`. Split that complement by choosing hues near it, commonly:

- `H`
- `H + 150`
- `H + 210`

The exact offsets can be adjusted for the color wheel, brand constraints, and desired contrast. Wrap values outside the 0 to 360 range.

## Character

- Contrasting, flexible, and easier to balance than direct complements.
- Supports a clear dominant color with two related accents.
- Offers variety without requiring three evenly distributed color families.

## Web Use

- Make the base hue dominant across primary actions, navigation, or branding.
- Use the two split-complement hues for secondary actions, illustrations, charts, or selected states.
- Keep the accent hues smaller in area or lower in chroma when the interface feels busy.
- Use neutral surfaces and deliberate lightness steps to keep the palette readable.
- Do not assign both accents to competing primary actions unless the interaction model makes the distinction clear.

## Example Brief

> Build a split-complementary palette around blue. Use blue as the dominant hue, with warm yellow-orange and red-orange accents near its complement. Keep the accents limited to calls to action and data highlights, and verify all text and control states.

## Common Mistake

Do not treat the two accent hues as interchangeable. They may be related geometrically, but each still needs a clear semantic or visual role.
